### 2.4 Study notes

These notes restate the course's main editing rules in new sentences. The examples were written for this repository. They are not lines from the lectures or from the article. The substitutions in unit 1 and unit 2 are the ones the article says the course repeats; the same pairs are listed again, with sources, in [`clutter-words.md`](clutter-words.md).

**Unit 1. Delete words that do not change the claim.**

Read the sentence and mark every word that could go without changing who did what, to what, and with what result. Then delete those words.

Common cuts the article attributes to this unit:

| Longer wording | Shorter wording |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| at the present time | now |
| has the potential to / has the ability to | can |
| a sufficient number of | enough |
| in spite of the fact that | although |

Also delete empty openers that add no finding: "It was observed that", "It is well known that". If the sentence still needs a hedge, use one verb of the right strength (see the writing skill's hedging table), not a stack of openers.

**Unit 2. Put the real action in the verb.**

A frequent pattern is a thin verb plus a noun that hides the action: "conduct an evaluation of", "perform a measurement of", "achieve an improvement in". The action is already in the noun. Use that word as the verb: evaluate, measure, improve. The subject and the verb then sit next to each other, and the chain of "of" / "by" gets shorter.

**Unit 2. Use the active voice when the actor is part of the claim.**

For work the author team did, "We measured" and "We found" name the actor and are usually shorter than "It was measured". Module 2 has a separate video on "we" and "I". Passive remains useful when the actor is not the point. The usual case in a paper is a method step in which the procedure matters more than which person ran it. Section 4 records how Nature's current page, Nature's Masterclasses post, and this repository's own Methods note say the same thing. Check the target journal before making every sentence active.

**Units 3 and 4, in one pass.** One sentence should carry one claim, plus the conditions that belong with that claim. Do not shorten a sentence by deleting the instrument, the time point, or the estimate. Do shorten it by deleting the empty words from unit 1. Paragraphs need a point in the first sentence. Revision is a separate step from the first draft. The page's module 4 says this directly.

**Unit 5. What each part of an original paper has to do.** The article's summary of this unit, restated:

- Introduction. Move from what the field already accepts, to the gap or the conflicting findings, to this paper's question. The introduction's job is to leave the reader with that unanswered question. A list of papers is not yet an introduction.
- Methods. Another lab should be able to repeat the work. Split the section by time or by experiment. Do not discuss, and do not put results in Methods.
- Results. Write the finding, then the estimate (effect, difference, or P) in parentheses, then the figure or table number. Do not read the table out as a second table.
- Discussion. Start by answering the question the introduction asked. Then compare with earlier studies and give a reason for agreement or disagreement. Then state the limits of this study. Then say what the result means beyond this sample.

Module 5 on Coursera also covers tables, figures, and the abstract, in the video order listed in §2.3. The article's unit 5 does not expand on those two.

**A worked pass on a sport-science sentence.** Original example, not a course excerpt:

> An evaluation of countermovement jump height was conducted using a force plate in order to achieve the determination of lower-limb power.

Three separate problems: the actions sit inside "evaluation" and "determination", the actor is missing, and "in order to" adds no meaning. One revision:

> We measured countermovement jump height on a force plate to determine lower-limb power.

Results, same rules:

> It was observed that there was an increase in session RPE after the congested fixture week.

> Session RPE increased after the congested fixture week.

Put the size of the increase, the comparison, and the figure number in the same sentence when you have them. Do not invent them to make the sentence look finished.

Discussion, same rules:

> The findings of the present study are indicative of the fact that the cooling vest has the ability to lower core temperature.

> The cooling vest lowered core temperature at the end of the trial (mean difference and 95% CI here; Figure N).

**Later units.** Modules 6–8 on the current page cover authorship, plagiarism, predatory journals, peer review, review articles, grants, letters, personal statements, and writing for the public. Use them when the task is one of those genres. A research-paper edit can stop after modules 1, 2, and 5 and still have the rules this repository uses most often.

## 3. Academic Phrasebank

[Academic Phrasebank](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/) is a free site from the University of Manchester. [Dr John Morley's page](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/dr-john-morley/) on that site says he created it. He is listed there as Honorary Senior Lecturer in the University Centre for Academic English.

The [About page](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/about-academic-phrasebank/) says the phrases were taken from real academic texts. The original set came from 100 postgraduate dissertations at Manchester. Phrases from journal articles have been added since. Most entries have had the study-specific words removed, so the line can be reused. The home page says that reuse of these generic lines is not plagiarism, and that a line still has to be adapted. Where a sample content word is left in, you replace it.

The About page cites Davis and Morley on how much wording academics were willing to see reused. The limits they report there: the reused stretch should not be a distinctive construction, should not carry another writer's view, was judged more acceptable up to about nine words, and could hold up to about four generic content words. Those are findings from that work, not a legal rule. This repository's citation rule is stricter for claims and numbers: a phrase frame does not license a finding you did not make.

On 3 November 2026 the home page had no PDF download. The article cites a 2014 PDF edition. Use the website. Do not pick up a PDF from an unrelated host.

The site has two menus.

**By part of the paper** (top menu):

| You are writing | Open | What to use the page for |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | [Introducing work](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/introducing-work/) | Context, the problem or gap, definitions, the purpose of the paper, a map of the paper |
| Literature | [Referring to sources](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/referring-to-sources/) | How to point the reader at earlier studies. The page notes that in science the author's name is often not the grammatical subject; a number or a bracket is enough. It also notes tense: present perfect for the literature as a whole, past for one completed study |
| Methods | [Describing methods](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/describing-methods/) | A repeatable account of what was done. The page says most of these verbs are in the simple past |
| Results | [Reporting results](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/reporting-results/) | Point at a table or figure, then state the data that matter. Longer comment belongs in the discussion. The page says some journals combine Results and Discussion |
| Discussion | [Discussing findings](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/discussing-findings/) | Restate a result, compare, explain, note a limit, state an implication. The page says the explanation and implication lines are tentative, and it points at the cautious-language page |
| Conclusion | [Writing conclusions](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/writing-conclusions/) | A short look back, then a closing judgment. The page says a separate conclusion is expected more often in a dissertation than in a research article |

**How to use a page on a sport-science draft**

1. Open the page for the section you are in. Read the headings and decide what the next sentence has to do (state a gap, name a procedure, report a difference, mark a limit).
2. Take a short frame if you need the grammar. Replace every sample noun. Keep it near the length Davis and Morley discuss. Do not paste a whole line and then drop in one variable name.
3. Run the unit 1–2 pass on that sentence. Phrasebank lines often still contain "it has been shown that" and similar openers. Cut those after the frame has done its job.
4. Put this study's facts in the sentence: who was tested, what the protocol was, what was measured, when, and how large the result was. A finished sentence that could move unchanged into another paper is not done. That test is [`human-voice-en.md` §1](../../../skills/sportsci-writing/references/human-voice-en.md).
5. For a hedge, pick one level from the cautious-language page, then match it to the evidence with [`human-voice-en.md` §3](../../../skills/sportsci-writing/references/human-voice-en.md). One hedge. "May potentially suggest" is three.
6. For criticism of earlier work, name the method or the result you are comparing. A general line such as "previous studies have failed to address" needs a specific missing piece, or it fails the same any-paper test.

Order of operations that matches both sources: Phrasebank for the job of the sentence, then the course rules to cut empty words and restore the verb, then the skill's specificity and hedging checks before the paragraph is finished.

## 4. Nature and Science on active voice

The article cites two pages. They were both opened on 3 November 2026. They do not currently say what a reader might expect from the article's wording alone.

### Nature

Current author page: [How to write your paper](https://www.nature.com/nature-portfolio/for-authors/write) (Nature Portfolio).

The sentence on that page on the check date:

> Write clear and direct sentences. Using the active voice ("we performed the experiment...") typically helps readers better understand concepts and results described in a paper.

The page the article cites is the Nature Masterclasses post [Writing for Greater Impact](https://www.nature.com/masterclasses/writing-for-greater-impact/50732650) (the post's own heading continues "How to Improve Readability"). The byline there is Dr Jeffrey Robens, Head of Community Engagement at Nature Portfolio. That post attributes this older wording to Nature:

> Nature journals prefer authors to write in the active voice as experience has shown that readers find concepts and results to be conveyed more clearly if written directly.

That exact sentence was not on the current "How to write your paper" page on the check date. The Masterclasses post still prints it as Nature's position. Use the current author page for what Nature Portfolio tells authors today, and the Masterclasses post for the longer explanation the article is pointing at.

The same post says the passive is still useful in four situations. Restated briefly:

- The data do not identify a cause. A passive, or a verb that does not claim a cause, keeps a correlation from being written as an effect. The post's example is LDL cholesterol and a Mediterranean diet.
- Methods, when the technique is the point rather than the person who ran it. The article's note that Nature allows the passive in Methods comes from this post, not from the short current author page.
- The known information needs to sit at the start of the next sentence so the paragraph can move forward.
- Occasional variation. The post suggests a rough mix of about 75% active and 25% passive. This repository does not use that ratio. Follow the target journal and the skill files.

The same post also tells writers to prefer a verb to a nominalization ("assess" rather than "assessment"), to cut words that add no meaning, and to avoid "it is well known that", "as a matter of fact", and "it is worth mentioning that". It suggests replacing "it is interesting to note that" with "interestingly" or "notably". Do not make that last substitution in a draft edited with this repository: [`ai-traces-en.md`](../../../skills/sportsci-writing/references/ai-traces-en.md) treats those sentence-opening adverbs as fillers to delete. Delete the opener and keep the fact.

### Science

The article cites [Instructions for preparing an initial manuscript](https://www.science.org/content/page/instructions-preparing-initial-manuscript) and says that page tells authors to use the active voice when suitable, with the example "we constructed a library" rather than "a library was constructed".

On 3 November 2026 that page is about file format, section order, figures, and Science citation style. The fetched text does not contain an active-voice rule or the library example.

The sentence the article is remembering is quoted elsewhere as older Science guidance. [MedSci Communications](https://medscicommunications.com/2019/12/18/is-passive-voice-passe/) quotes it and cites `http://www.sciencemag.org/about/authors/prep/res/style.dtl`, accessed 4 March 2009. The wording in that quotation is: use the active voice when suitable, particularly when the syntax needs it, for example "To address this possibility, we constructed a λZap library …" rather than "To address this possibility, a λZap library was constructed …". A 2017 set of learner notes on module 2.2 of this course quotes the same Science lines: [Kian Meng's week 2 notes](https://www.kianmeng.org/2017/09/coursera-writing-in-sciences-week-2.html). Those are secondary quotations of an older style page. They are not the current initial-manuscript instructions.

Until Science puts the rule back on a current author page, do not cite the 2026 instructions URL as the source of that sentence. For a paper aimed at *Science*, read the current instructions for format, and read recent *Science* papers for voice.

Practical order on a draft you are about to run through `ai_trace_check.py`:

1. Cut empty words and restore verbs (units 1–2, or [`clutter-words.md`](clutter-words.md) if you want the list).
2. Decide active or passive for each sentence from the journal and from whether the actor is part of the claim.
3. If a sentence's grammar is the problem, look up the Phrasebank page for that section, then fill it with this study.
4. Run `python skills/sportsci-writing/scripts/ai_trace_check.py draft.md` from the repository root. Fix hits. Read warnings in context.
5. Do the positive check in `human-voice-en.md` §10 by reading. The script cannot see missing sample size, missing instruments, or a hedge that is the same on every claim.
